# Mathematics: Romanticisation to Industrialisation

On the 6th of October 2026, OpenAI published the modestly named “math” repository on GitHub, containing ~722 manuscripts claiming proofs of long-standing problems, with Lean formalised proofs for many of them. These claims spanned the mathematical diaspora, from proofs in mathematical physics to number theory. Notably, this included a proof of the Quasi-Riemann hypothesis, which narrows the bound on the real part of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function to the interval [1/8, 7/8]. While this does not prove the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) itself, the claim that all the zeros of the Riemann zeta function have real part equal to ½, Terry Tao claims this is the greatest breakthrough in the quest to solve what is arguably the most prestigious open problem left in mathematics. He also claims it likely would’ve taken at least 50 human years for the community to prove this. Taking this idea further, I estimated it would’ve taken just south of 1000 human years to produce a similarly sized dump of manuscripts. **Is this the end of a human-powered age of blackboard proofs, and the beginning of industrial machine-powered mathematics?**

Some of the responses of the maths community have been equally astounding. French mathematician and 2022 Fields Medallist, Hugo Duminil-Copin, claimed that upon the OpenAI dump event, he cried so loudly it woke up his young daughter. Among the ~722 manuscripts were proofs to 16 open problems Hugo was working on. Such feeling was likely expressed across many math departments. As I digested what this event meant, I weirdly landed on the question of why I chose to study mathematics. For those that don’t know me, I am quite a social person, and dodgy online personality tests tell me I am an extrovert. Fundamentally, in the dichotomy between interest in people and things, I subscribe to the people camp. Thus, it is somewhat surprising that I was deciding between studying maths and physics or engineering at university.

My passion for mathematics and physics only really began in the final year of high school through doing hundreds of partial fractions and integrals in the COVID lockdown and obsessing over my physics classes delivered excellently by Mr Ferguson. Much of our homework set was watching various videos produced by famous YouTubers such as 3Blue1Brown, Veritasium and the Physics Girl, covering topics like special relativity, Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, linear algebra and black holes. One lesson discussed wave-particle duality, a fascinating idea that suggests all matter can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like behaviours depending on how the matter is observed, and seemingly, what mood it is in. Not only did the idea amaze me, but also the story of its creation. The young Frenchman Louis De Broglie suggested the audacious idea in his PhD thesis of 1924, which, as myth has it, was only 3 pages long. A single Google search reveals this to be false, but it certainly made a good story and had captured my attention. Nonetheless, the discovery had cemented De Broglie’s name in history, and the story had captured my imagination of producing a similarly monumental discovery and securing eternal fame.

This fascination with not only ideas but the stories of the people behind them was not unique to De Broglie, but also to others: Galois, a French aristocrat who died in a duel aged 20, fighting for the hand of a lady; Ramanujan, with his natural genius and distaste for English food; and Paul Erdős, with his relentless work ethic and amphetamine use. Arguably, this focus on individuals and their success is something relatively unique in the 21st century to maths amongst the sciences. Whilst other sciences work in large research groups, mathematicians typically publish papers with at most half a dozen collaborators. Similarly, the tools of mathematicians have largely not changed for at least the last 200 years, with blackboards still being installed in newly built mathematics departments around the world. In some sense, the arrival of AI for maths is like giving a Tudor peasant an Alexa.

So, as mathematics leaves its romantic era, the final modern science to do so, and enters an industrial one, more success will be seen by swarms of AI agents, and less by human geniuses; with it, the reduction in myths and legends of mathematical heroes, hence, losing part of its attraction. As Terence Tao puts it, we are entering the era of “No more heroes” in maths. Maybe this is a good thing. Maybe this leads to more collaborative projects and fewer lone geniuses. Maybe it produces an exponential expansion in human knowledge. These are all possibilities. However, what I know for certain is that part of the attraction of maths is lost forever. No longer will we celebrate the Andrew Wiles-like figure who works secretively for years; instead, we will take bets on whether it is a swarm of Anthropic or OpenAI agents that have solved the next big open problem.

## So what now?

Ok, so what happens next? As of 22 Nov. 26, two millennium problems have been solved. In some sense, it is the most exciting time to be in mathematics. Not only will AI continue to solve more open problems; it's likely it will begin to propose even deeper questions that will accelerate the human understanding of maths and the world around us into the realm of things we didn’t know we didn’t know. However, I have some concerns about the trajectories of the AI and math partnership. I will likely blog about these more in the future in more detail, but here I am just outlining some things I am most concerned about.

**Firstly, mathematicians do not own their own future.** Before the OpenAI dump, not a single mathematician was pre-warned or consulted before their life’s work was completely upended. Whilst anyone has the right to publish a solution to any given open problem, OpenAI and Anthropic have numerous other interests and reasons to publish these solutions that aren’t to further the extent of human knowledge. These are companies at the end of the day, whose priority is to make a return and drive profits, particularly during a flotation (IPO) in the case of Anthropic. I am not convinced they have the maths community's best interests at heart. This is turning into a dangerous dick-swinging contest between OpenAI and Anthropic, with maths being the poor platform in which this is happening.

**Secondly, there are ownership issues.** It is not clear if the AI solutions, particularly in the case of Navier-Stokes, were completely original. The mathematics community has always been extremely careful when it comes to recognising the ownership of ideas with collaborators. If it does turn out that the Navier-Stokes proof contained ideas leaked from private conversations, which were not properly recognised, then that is a serious issue. Similarly, the Riemann Hypothesis release from Levent Alpöge consists of a 250,000-page PDF and 17 million lines of Lean code, which will certainly contain hundreds of both published and possibly unpublished methods and ideas. How do we ensure everyone is properly recognised for their contributions? What about work leaking from personal use into training data, and can proper ownership be assigned? After speaking to a former lecturer at my graduation this week, he said he was genuinely concerned about the latter. He has been using an internal Edinburgh Uni LLM that can plug into Claude, ChatGPT, etc. that ensures no leakage of ideas.

**Thirdly, what to do with the slop.** As I stated above, the RH release contained a quarter of a million pages, though it is still shorter than the whopping 359,866 page-long planning application for the lower Thames crossing (I love British planning applications). Nonetheless, 250,000 is still far beyond something digestible, particularly for a community that sees presentation as just as important as the ideas being conveyed. When discussing complex ideas, you do not want to make the reader's life more complicated with poor presentation and communication. The presentation of these results has been of concern in the community and makes understanding them all the more difficult. We must give ourselves a chance at understanding these proofs by at least producing something we can read and begin to work through.

**Finally, there seems to be some confusion of why we care about mathematics.** If maths is purely the pursuit of completing all open problems, proposing more complicated ones until every possible theorem is proposed and proved, then yes, AI is wonderful, and we can let it go along this path of ‘completing’ (if maths were finite) maths. However, this misses the point I think most of us would agree on: that human understanding is fundamental. As has been made clear, the exciting part of breakthroughs is usually not the result itself, but the new techniques and methods used in the proof, which can open many more avenues, invent new ideas, and provide tools for proving other open problems. This must be set as a standard for all AI-assisted proofs. All intermediate steps and methods used need to be explained, digestible to humans and, as stated above, clearly presented.

---

This blog has dragged on a bit and is a cocktail of thoughts and ideas I have been having. It has been helpful in gathering my personal thoughts in these choppy waters, and will serve as a nice personal reference back to my feelings during one of the most turbulent times for mathematicians. Above, I have been particularly critical and pessimistic; however, I am generally quite optimistic about the future. I think we will find new ways to work alongside this technology, and weirdly, think it will finally make mathematics a more collaborative endeavour. If I can be bothered, I will continue to write about this topic, hopefully in a more positive tone. However, the main point of this blog has been to highlight what I see as a hidden loss in all of this chaos, namely, the stories of heroes and individuals in maths being lost and how this contributes to its attractiveness as a subject. I fear many prospective maths students may simply ask, “What is the point?” and turn away from mathematics.
